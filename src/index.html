Your Feelings

Having T1D can bring up all sorts of feelings, and pretty much all of them are completely normal. It's okay to feel annoyed or fed up with it sometimes, even on days when nothing's actually gone wrong. And if you ever worry that you're a burden to the people looking after you, you're not. Looking after you is something they want to do.

What's that beep? 🔔

Sometimes your kit might beep or buzz, that's totally normal! It's just your gadget saying "Hey, just checking in!" It's not broken, and you haven't done anything wrong. It happens to loads of kids every single day.

Working with School

School is where a lot of the day happens without you there. Getting the basics set up early, and revisited at the right moments, makes a real difference to how smoothly things run.

Tap a topic to find out more.

Worth sharing with school

  • A written care plan, agreed with the diabetes team, covering day-to-day management and emergency steps
  • Who to contact, and in what order, if something needs a decision quickly
  • What a low or high might look like for this child specifically, since it doesn't always match the textbook
  • Any equipment kept on site, and who knows how to help if it's needed
  • A plan for PE, trips, and exams, agreed ahead of time rather than worked out on the day

Starting a new school year, a new teacher, or a new school entirely means going through the basics again, even if it feels repetitive. A quick refresh conversation each time is worth the effort.

Just Diagnosed

If your child has just been diagnosed with T1D, you're probably still taking it in. Whatever you're feeling right now, and however much you're feeling it, that's a normal response to a lot of new information landing at once. You don't need to have it figured out today.

A new normal will gradually settle in, it rarely feels this overwhelming for long. And when you're talking to your diabetes team, no question is a silly one, if it's on your mind, it's worth asking.

It gets easier

Nearly everything about this feels unfamiliar right now, the terminology, the routine, the equipment. Almost all of it becomes second nature faster than it seems possible today. You're not expected to become an expert overnight, your diabetes team is there for exactly that reason, and you can lean on them as much as you need to.

You don't have to navigate this alone. Peer support groups, like Go Type 1!, connect families going through the same thing, often the most reassuring conversations happen with someone else who's already been where you are now.

The Basics

Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune condition where the body's immune system stops the pancreas from making insulin. It is not caused by diet or lifestyle, and it cannot be prevented or cured, yet. A huge amount of advances are being made all the time.

Insulin is the hormone that lets sugar (glucose) move from the blood into cells for energy. Without it, blood glucose rises. People with T1D manage this with insulin delivered by injection or pump, guided by their diabetes care team.

Blood glucose can run high (hyperglycaemia) or low (hypoglycaemia), and both can affect mood, concentration and behaviour.

Understanding the Equipment

There's usually a fair bit of new kit to get your head around early on. This is a plain-language overview of what each device is and roughly what to expect day to day, not a recommendation of any particular option. Which equipment is used, and when, is a decision for the child's diabetes team based on what suits them. Your diabetes team will always help explain everything properly, hands-on, so you never have to figure it out alone.

Tap a device to see what to expect.

A few practical things worth knowing

  • Sharps (needles, lancets) need a proper sharps bin, your pharmacy or diabetes team can tell you how to get one and how to arrange collection or disposal
  • It's worth keeping a small backup kit (spare pen, strips, batteries) somewhere reliable, like a school bag or car, in case the primary device fails
  • Travelling, especially by air, usually needs a letter from the diabetes team confirming the equipment and supplies are medically necessary
  • Devices occasionally fail, come loose, or give an unexpected reading, this is common and usually has a straightforward fix, your diabetes team's advice line is there for exactly this

If anything about the equipment feels confusing or overwhelming, that's completely normal, it's a lot to take in. Their Team can talk you through the specifics, show you how everything works hands-on, and help you find what suits your family best.

Looking After You

Supporting a child with T1D is a marathon, not a sprint. How you're doing affects how well you can show up for them, so this isn't indulgent, it's part of the job.

A minute to reset

Tap start when you're ready

A simple box-breathing pattern: in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4.

Small things that may genuinely help

  1. Make tiny pockets of calm. Take a minute to breathe while the kettle boils or sit quietly after a sensor change. Small pauses help your mind reset.
  2. Keep one simple routine for yourself. A morning drink, a good book, gentle swim. Something that stays the same even when diabetes feels unpredictable.
  3. Share tasks when you can. Let someone else handle bedtime, school runs or kit organising when possible.
  4. Rest in real ways. If nights are broken, sleep whenever you can. Naps, early nights can make a big difference.
  5. Have moments where diabetes is not the topic. A meal, a walk, a chat or a TV episode where numbers and alarms are not discussed. Your mind needs breaks.
  6. Keep food and water easy for yourself. Simple meals, snacks you enjoy and a water bottle you actually use help keep you healthy.
  7. Talk to someone who understands T1D life. Another parent, a nurse or a friend who listens.
  8. Write things down when your head feels full. Not for organisation, but for relief. Putting worries on paper can help.
  9. Notice the good moments.
  10. Looking after yourself is part of looking after your child. A steadier you makes diabetes feel lighter for them.

Permission to rest

Asking for a break doesn't mean you're failing at this. Accepting help from someone else doesn't mean you're not capable. Having an off day doesn't undo all the good days. Care that isn't sustainable for you isn't sustainable for them either.

Building your support network

  • Teach the basics to one other trusted adult, a partner, grandparent, or regular babysitter, so you're never the only one who knows what to do
  • Put together a one-page brief for schools, sleepovers, or clubs so you're not re-explaining everything from scratch each time
  • Look for a peer support group in your area, connecting with other families who understand it day-to-day makes a real difference

One thing that went okay today

It's easy to only notice what went wrong. Jot down one small win before you finish up today.

When to reach out for extra support

If things feel like more than day-to-day stress, if you're feeling low most of the time, or it's affecting your sleep, health, or relationships in a lasting way, it's worth talking to your GP, a health visitor, or a counsellor. Reaching out early tends to help more than waiting until things feel unmanageable.
